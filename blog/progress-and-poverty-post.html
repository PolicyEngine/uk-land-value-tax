How replacing council tax with a flat land value tax would affect households in the UK

By Vahid Ahmadi and Max Ghenis, PolicyEngine · May 2026

Britain's valuers had about two years to band every home in Great Britain, more than 20 million properties. Environment Secretary Michael Heseltine told the Commons on 21 March 1991 that the new tax would arrive in 1993; the Local Government Finance Act 1992 brought council tax into force on 1 April 1993, replacing Margaret Thatcher's poll tax after riots and mass non-payment had killed it.

To meet the deadline, the Inland Revenue's Valuation Office and the Scottish Assessors brought in estate agents who drove down streets banding houses without getting out of the car, a practice critics later called "second-gear valuations". Each property went into one of eight bands, with Band H, the top band in England, covering every home worth £320,000 or more in 1991 prices.

That snapshot is still the law. England and Scotland have never been revalued; the government cancelled a planned 2007 review before it ran. Wales rebanded in 2005 from a 2003 valuation date, and Northern Ireland uses a separate domestic rates system based on 2005 valuations. Because the top band caps at £320,000 in 1991 prices, the council tax falls as a share of property value for higher-priced homes. That pattern has driven reform proposals to tie property taxation more closely to current property or land values from the Mirrlees Review, the Institute for Fiscal Studies, John Muellbauer, and the Fairer Share campaign.

The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) forecasts council tax to raise £53.7 billion across Great Britain in the 2026-27 fiscal year. Low-income households can apply for Council Tax Reduction (CTR), a means-tested local rebate that lowers a household's bill against the council tax it would otherwise owe. Local authorities in England design their own CTR schemes; the Scottish and Welsh governments set national ones. Northern Ireland uses a separate domestic rates system, which this analysis leaves unchanged.

This post simulates replacing council tax with a flat land value tax (LVT) that raises the same revenue, and reports the household-level impact at the budget-neutral rate.

What we find

  • The UK could replace council tax with a budget-neutral 0.77% LVT on its £7.4 trillion of land value.
  • 68% of households would have higher net income. The bottom income decile gains £522 a year on average; the ninth decile pays £1,011 more (the largest average cash loss), and the top decile pays £967 more.
  • Absolute poverty before housing costs falls by 0.57 percentage points.
  • The income Gini coefficient rises by 0.58%.

If some of these results look contradictory or surprising, they did to us too — and we hope walking through them in the rest of the post makes the case for carefully picking and defining how to measure a policy's impacts.

The model

We run this analysis with PolicyEngine UK, an open-source microsimulation, on Enhanced Family Resources Survey 2023-24 microdata.1

On the council tax side, FRS households report their council tax band (A–H, plus I in Wales). The survey asks for the gross annual bill; about a quarter of households give an amount. For the rest, we impute the mean of those reported amounts for the household's region × band × single-person-status cell, which captures the band rate and the standard single-person discount. Council Tax Reduction itself comes from FRS benefit responses — recipients tell the survey how much CTR they receive — and stays separate from the bill imputation. For the synthetic high-income observations the Enhanced FRS adds to cover the top of the income distribution, a quantile random forest re-predicts CTR (alongside the other FRS-only benefit reports) from demographics and the imputed income components.3 FRS-aggregated bills nevertheless fall short of published council tax totals, so we calibrate household weights against the Valuation Office Agency stock-of-properties counts of dwellings by band and region (Scotland from the Scottish Government chargeable-dwellings dataset) and against Office for Budget Responsibility council tax receipts. We model Northern Ireland's domestic rates separately, and they remain in place under this reform. The 2026-27 baseline this post replaces — gross billing minus CTR — is £57.3 billion, modestly above the OBR's £53.7 billion accruals-basis forecast because PolicyEngine's billing-side calibration matches dwelling stock counts and modelled CTR uptake rather than realised receipts.

On the land side, the model gives each household a directly held land-value component and distributes aggregate corporate land to households in proportion to their corporate wealth. We calibrate the total to the Office for National Statistics (ONS) National Balance Sheet2 (2024 target: £7.10 trillion) and uprate to 2026-27 using per-capita nominal GDP growth from the OBR March 2025 Economic and Fiscal Outlook, producing a £7.43 trillion total: £5.37tn held directly by households and £2.06tn through the corporate sector.

The model reports revenue, distributional, poverty, and inequality outcomes at any LVT rate, for every income decile and family type. All figures cover the 2026-27 fiscal year (April 2026 to March 2027).

The reform

The reform abolishes council tax across Great Britain and replaces it with a flat LVT on all UK land value at the rate that holds aggregate council tax revenue constant. Northern Ireland's domestic rates remain in place; Northern Ireland households therefore face the land value tax on top of their unchanged rates bill. The LVT applies to all land, including the £2.06 trillion held through the corporate sector, so commercial land contributes alongside residential, while business rates on commercial property also stay in place. For distributional results, the model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners: directly for household-held land, and indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land. No other taxes or benefits change.

Abolishing council tax retires the Council Tax Reduction rebate alongside it — there is no council tax for CTR to rebate. Households who currently receive CTR therefore lose the rebate when they lose the bill, and their out-of-pocket position changes by net council tax minus LVT, not gross council tax minus LVT. A future variant could preserve a CTR-equivalent benefit funded centrally; in that case bottom-decile gains would rise modestly because CTR recipients would keep the rebate cash while no longer paying any council tax.

Revenue and rate sensitivity

PolicyEngine UK estimates £57.3 billion per year from current council tax (net of Council Tax Reduction). Dividing that by £7.43 trillion of UK land value gives the budget-neutral rate of 0.77%. Table 1 reports outcomes at three illustrative rates: 0.5%, 0.77%, and 1.0%.

0.77% (neutral)£068%−0.57 pp+0.58%
1.0%+£16.9bn61%+0.30 pp+1.59%

Below the budget-neutral rate, revenue falls and the share of households with higher net income rises. At 0.5%, the model raises £20 billion less than current council tax and 78% of households gain. Above the budget-neutral rate, revenue rises and the share of gainers falls. At 1.0%, the reform raises £17bn more and 61% gain. We hold the rate range to 0.5%–1.0% because the static model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners and does not capture land-price capitalisation, rent pass-through, or behavioural responses, all of which matter more at higher annual rates.

For context, the baseline 2026-27 Gini coefficient on income is 0.38, while the Gini on household total wealth is 0.71. Wealth in the UK is far more concentrated than income; the static model leaves wealth stocks unchanged under the reform.

Every income decile from 1 through 8 gains on average; only deciles 9 and 10 lose. The bottom decile gains £522 a year (2.6% of net income); decile 9 takes the largest cash loss at £1,011 (1.4% of net income), with the top decile paying £967 more (0.7% of net income). The pattern across the lower deciles is uneven because income and land holdings are imperfectly correlated: even where the average gains, a high-land minority within the decile pays more under the LVT than under current council tax. Figure 4 plots the same impact in pounds per year.

Figure 4: Average net income change by income decile, 2026-27 (£/year)

Across all households, 68% have higher net income at the budget-neutral rate. Absolute poverty before housing costs falls by 0.57 percentage points. The income Gini coefficient rises by 0.58% — small, but in the opposite direction of the headline gain pattern. The mechanism: LVT redistributes along the wealth axis, not the income axis, so within every income decile, land-rich households (often older homeowners with paid-off properties) pay much more while land-poor households (renters, recent buyers) pay less. That within-decile crisscrossing widens the income spread enough to nudge the Gini up, even as decile averages move progressively.

Same reform, by wealth decile

Wealth deciles cut the same simulation along a different axis. Figure 5 plots the average net income change at the 0.77% LVT, by household wealth decile rather than income decile.

Figure 5: Average net income change by wealth decile, 2026-27 (£/year)

The wealth-decile picture is cleaner than the income-decile one. Wealth deciles 2–7 all gain (between £483 and £1,454 a year on average); only deciles 8, 9, and 10 lose, with the top wealth decile paying £5,539 more on average, a far larger swing than the £967 average loss for the top income decile. (Decile 1 is empty in this chart: roughly 10% of households hold no land or housing wealth at all and fall outside PolicyEngine UK's wealth-decile binning, which starts at decile 2 in this dataset. Those households would gain under the reform — they pay no LVT and save their council tax — but they sit outside the chart.) The reform is, by construction, a tax on land wealth. It lands progressively along the wealth distribution even when the income-decile pattern is noisier.

The Mirrlees Review and the Fairer Share proposal both discuss deferral mechanisms (letting households accrue the LVT charge against the property until sale or inheritance) as one design response for the income-poor, land-rich households the income-decile averages obscure. PolicyEngine UK can score any of these designs (deferrals, transitional caps, age-based reliefs, or income-tested reductions) with the same distributional metrics; however, we exclude them for this analysis.

Who gains and loses, within deciles

Decile averages hide variation within deciles. Figure 6 reports the share of households with higher, lower, or unchanged net income at the 0.77% rate, by income decile.

Figure 6: Share of households with higher, lower, and unchanged net income by income decile, 0.77% LVT replacing council tax, 2026-27 (%)

In the top decile, 49% of households still have higher net income, despite the average loss of £967; in the bottom decile, 21% have lower net income, despite the average gain of £522. The share of losing households is around 21–28% in deciles 1–4 and climbs to roughly half by deciles 9 and 10, with non-monotonic dips in between. The same high-land-value, lower-income households visible in the loss share across deciles 1–8 are the group most LVT designs target with deferrals or transitional protection.

What this model doesn't capture

The static model assigns the first-round LVT burden to current landowners (directly for household-held land, indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land), and holds everything else constant. It does not model land-price capitalisation; rent pass-through to tenants; behavioural responses in land use and development decisions; or the redesign of devolved local-government funding that any reform would require. The model also abstracts from implementation: who values the land, how often, and how to resolve disputes. And because LVT depends on land value rather than income, replacing council tax with an LVT removes the income-tested phase-out that CTR imposes on low-income households, reducing their effective marginal tax rates — an incentive effect the static model does not score.

Each is a model extension, not an alternative analysis. The directions PolicyEngine UK is investigating: fuller local-authority calibration; capitalisation modelling that tracks burden over time; transitional designs (deferrals, phased introduction, age- or income-based protection); and rent-pass-through under alternative supply-elasticity assumptions.

Using the model

PolicyEngine UK is open source. The model scores specific designs (phased introduction, pensioner deferrals, multi-rate by region, alternative bases) in the same format as this analysis. The code and underlying data are public, and anyone can reproduce these results.
