How replacing council tax with a flat land value tax would affect households in the UK

By Vahid Ahmadi, PolicyEngine · April 2026

Council tax in England and Scotland is still calculated using property valuations from April 1991, capped at a top band (Band H) that originally covered homes worth £320,000 or more in 1991 prices (Wales rebanded in 2005 from a 2003 valuation date; Northern Ireland uses a separate domestic rates system based on 2005 valuations). Because the bands compress at the top, council tax falls as a share of property value above the lower bands. The Mirrlees Review, the IFS, John Muellbauer, and the Fairer Share campaign have all proposed reforms that link property taxation more closely to current property or land values.

This post uses PolicyEngine UK, an open-source household-level microsimulation, to model one option researchers have put forward: replacing council tax with a flat land value tax (LVT). The model runs at any rate. PolicyEngine UK estimates that the rate which holds revenue constant is 0.83% on all UK land value, raising the same £61.4 billion that council tax (and the equivalent Northern Ireland domestic rates) raise today. At that rate, 71% of households would have higher net income, the bottom income decile would gain £864 a year on average, and the ninth decile would pay £1,205 more (the largest average loss in cash terms), with the top decile paying £1,163 more. Below: how the model builds the LVT base, what rate sensitivity looks like, the distributional pattern by decile, and where the static modelling reaches its limits.

What we find

  • UK land value: £7.44 trillion in 2026-27, 4.8% above the ONS 2024 aggregate after OBR uprating. Households hold £5.38tn directly and £2.06tn through the corporate sector. Directly held land equals 69% of UK property wealth; total land equals 28% of household wealth.
  • Budget-neutral rate: 0.83% on all UK land value matches £61.4 billion of council tax revenue.
  • At 0.83%, 71% of households have higher net income, before-housing-costs relative poverty falls by 0.49 percentage points, and the Gini coefficient on equivalised household income rises by 0.0017.
  • Decile averages range from +£864 (decile 1) to −£1,205 (decile 9). Decile 4 is the exception in the lower half of the income distribution; the wealth-decile cut below shows why.

The model

PolicyEngine UK (version: 2.88.10) produces this analysis using its open-source static microsimulation built on the Enhanced Family Resources Survey 2023-24. Each household is assigned a directly held land-value component, and aggregate corporate land is attributed to households through their corporate wealth. The aggregate is calibrated to the ONS National Balance Sheet series AN.211 (2024 target: £7.1 trillion) and uprated to 2026-27 using per-capita nominal GDP growth from the OBR March 2025 Economic and Fiscal Outlook. The model reports revenue, distributional, poverty, and inequality outcomes at any LVT rate, for every income decile and family type. All figures are for the 2026-27 financial year. Code and reproducible results are linked at the bottom.

The reform

The reform abolishes council tax across Great Britain (and the equivalent domestic rates in Northern Ireland), replacing it with a flat LVT on all UK land value at the rate that maintains aggregate revenue. The LVT applies to all land — including the £2.06 trillion held through the corporate sector — so commercial land contributes alongside residential, while business rates on commercial property are unchanged. For distributional results, the model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners: directly for household-held land, and indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land. No other taxes or benefits change.

Revenue and rate sensitivity

PolicyEngine UK estimates £61.4 billion per year from current council tax (and Northern Ireland domestic rates). Dividing that by £7.44 trillion of UK land value gives the budget-neutral rate of 0.83%. Table 2 reports outcomes at three illustrative rates: 0.5%, 0.83%, and 1.0%.

Below the budget-neutral rate, revenue falls and the share of households with higher net income rises. At 0.5%, the model raises £24 billion less than current council tax and 82% of households gain. Above the budget-neutral rate, revenue rises and the share of gainers falls. At 1.0%, the reform raises £13bn more and 65% gain. We hold the rate range to 0.5%-1.0% because the static model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners and does not capture land-price capitalisation, rent pass-through, or behavioural responses — all of which matter more at higher annual rates.

Same reform, by wealth decile

Income deciles mix together households whose land holdings vary widely (the lower-income, high-land households that pull decile 4 negative are an example). Wealth deciles cut the same simulation along a different axis. Figure 5 plots the average net income change at the 0.83% LVT, by household wealth decile rather than income decile.

Figure 5: Average net income change by wealth decile, 2026-27 (£/year)

The wealth-decile picture is much cleaner than the income-decile one. The bottom seven wealth deciles all gain (between £480 and £1,519 a year on average); only deciles 8, 9, and 10 lose, with the top wealth decile paying £6,139 more on average — a far larger swing than the £1,163 average loss for the top income decile. The reform is, by construction, a tax on land wealth, so it lands progressively along the wealth distribution even where the income-decile pattern is muddier.

The Mirrlees Review and the Fairer Share proposal both discuss deferral mechanisms (letting households accrue the LVT charge against the property until sale or inheritance) as one design response for the income-poor, land-rich households the income-decile averages obscure. PolicyEngine UK can model deferrals, transitional caps, age-based reliefs, or income-tested reductions once a specific design is provided. The version reported here includes none of these.

Who gains and loses, within deciles

Decile averages hide variation within deciles. Figure 6 reports the share of households with higher, lower, or unchanged net income at the 0.83% rate, by income decile.

Figure 6: Share of households with higher, lower, and unchanged net income by income decile, 0.83% LVT replacing council tax, 2026-27 (%)

Within every decile, both gainers and losers exist. In the top decile, 48% of households still have higher net income, despite the average loss of £1,163; in the bottom decile, 10% have lower net income, despite the average gain of £864. The share of losing households is around 10-25% in deciles 1-4 and rises through deciles 5-10, jumping past half in deciles 9 and 10. The same high-land-value, lower-income households that drag decile 4 negative on average are visible across deciles 1-7 in the loss share, and are the group most policy designs in this space try to address with deferrals or transitional protection.

What this model doesn't capture

The static model assigns the first-round LVT burden to current landowners (directly for household-held land, indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land), and holds everything else constant. It does not model land-price capitalisation (an annual LVT lowers the present value of land, so part of the long-run burden falls on current owners as a one-off capital loss); rent pass-through to tenants (theoretically zero in textbook treatment, but empirically depends on local market structure and supply elasticity); behavioural responses in land use and development decisions; or the redesign of devolved local-government funding that any real reform would require. The model also abstracts from implementation: who values the land, how often, and how disputes are resolved.

Each is a model extension, not an alternative analysis. The directions PolicyEngine UK is investigating: fuller local-authority calibration; capitalisation modelling that tracks burden over time; transitional designs (deferrals, phased introduction, age- or income-based protection); and rent-pass-through under alternative supply-elasticity assumptions.

Using the model

PolicyEngine UK is open source and runs at any LVT rate, for every income decile and family type. Specific designs (phased introduction, pensioner deferrals, multi-rate by region, alternative bases) can be scored in the same format as this analysis. The code and underlying data are public; the results are reproducible.
