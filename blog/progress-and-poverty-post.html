Land value tax analysis in PolicyEngine UK: replacing council tax

By Vahid Ahmadi, PolicyEngine · April 2026

England and Scotland still base council tax on property valuations from April 1991 and cap bands at Band H (Wales revalued from 1 April 2005 using a 1 April 2003 valuation date; Northern Ireland uses 2005 valuations for its separate domestic rates system). Because the England and Scotland valuations are three decades old and the bands compress at the top, council tax falls as a share of property value for homes above the lower bands. The Mirrlees Review, the IFS, John Muellbauer, and the Fairer Share campaign have proposed reforms that move property taxation closer to current property or land values. This post models one option: replacing council tax with a flat land value tax (LVT).

PolicyEngine UK is a household-level microsimulation that estimates the effects of policy reforms for individual UK households and aggregate groups at any LVT rate. This post describes the model, presents the headline findings from a revenue-neutral swap of council tax for a flat LVT, and outlines where the model goes next.

Key findings

  • We estimate UK land value at £7.23 trillion in 2026-27. Directly held household land accounts for 67% of UK property wealth; total land, including corporate land attributed to households, accounts for 35% of household wealth.
  • We estimate that a flat LVT of 0.85% on all UK land value raises the same revenue as council tax (£61.2 billion).
  • We estimate that at this rate, 68% of households have higher net income. The bottom income decile gains £386/year on average; the top decile loses £1,035/year.

The PolicyEngine UK model

The sections below describe the simulation, the land-value data that forms the LVT base, and the distribution of land value across UK households. PolicyEngine UK 2.86.12 produced this analysis using its open-source static microsimulation model for UK tax and benefit reforms. The simulation uses the Enhanced Family Resources Survey 2023-24, attributes a directly held land-value component to every household, attributes aggregate corporate land to households through corporate wealth, calibrates the aggregate to the ONS National Balance Sheet (series AN.211, 2024 target of £7.1 trillion), and uprates to 2026-27 using per-capita nominal GDP growth from the OBR March 2025 Economic and Fiscal Outlook. The model reports revenue, distributional, poverty, and inequality outcomes at any LVT rate, for every income decile and family type. All figures are for the 2026-27 financial year.

The reform

The reform abolishes council tax (and Northern Ireland's equivalent domestic rates) and replaces it with a flat LVT on all UK land value, set at the revenue-neutral rate. For distributional results, the model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners: directly for household-held land, and indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land. No other taxes or benefits change. The parts below report outcomes at a range of LVT rates, the household-level distributional impact at the budget-neutral rate, and the share of households with higher net income.

Outcomes at different LVT rates

UK council tax (with Northern Ireland's equivalent domestic rates) raises £61.2 billion per year. Dividing that by £7.23 trillion of UK land value gives a budget-neutral LVT rate of 0.85%. Table 2 reports revenue (relative to the current baseline), the share of households with higher net income, the change in relative before-housing-costs poverty, and the change in the Gini coefficient at three illustrative rates: 0.5%, 0.85% (budget-neutral), and 1.0%.

At 0.5%, the reform raises £25 billion less than current council tax and 83% of households have higher net income. At 1.0%, it raises £11.1 billion more than current council tax revenue and 64% of households have higher net income. The analysis stays within the 0.5%-1.0% range because the static model does not capture land-price capitalisation, rent pass-through, or behavioural responses that become more important at higher annual rates.

Summary and next steps

A flat LVT of 0.85% on all UK land value raises the same revenue as council tax (£61.2 billion). At this rate, 68% of households have higher net income: the bottom income decile gains £386 per year on average, and the top decile loses £1,035 per year. Relative poverty falls by 0.9 percentage points before housing costs. The model runs at any LVT rate, for every income decile and family type.

The model is static and does not capture land-price capitalisation, rent pass-through, landlord behavioural responses, or local government redesign and devolved rate-setting. The model excludes long-run equilibrium effects. We plan to add fuller local-authority calibration, transitional policies (deferrals, phased introduction, protection for specific groups), a capitalisation model that tracks burden over time, and behavioural responses in land use and development decisions.
