How replacing council tax with a flat land value tax would affect households in the UK

By Vahid Ahmadi, PolicyEngine · May 2026

Britain's valuers had about two years to band every home in Great Britain, more than 20 million properties. Environment Secretary Michael Heseltine told the Commons on 21 March 1991 that the new tax would arrive in 1993; the Local Government Finance Act 1992 brought council tax into force on 1 April 1993, replacing Margaret Thatcher's poll tax after riots and mass non-payment had killed it.

To meet the deadline, the Inland Revenue's Valuation Office and the Scottish Assessors brought in estate agents who drove down streets banding houses without getting out of the car, a practice critics later called "second-gear valuations". Each property went into one of eight bands, with Band H, the top band in England, covering every home worth £320,000 or more in 1991 prices.

That snapshot is still the law. England and Scotland have never had a revaluation; the government cancelled a planned 2007 review before it ran. Wales rebanded in 2005 from a 2003 valuation date, and Northern Ireland uses a separate domestic rates system based on 2005 valuations. Because the top band caps at £320,000 in 1991 prices, the council tax shrinks as a share of value for higher-priced homes. This regressive shape has driven proposals to tie property taxation more closely to current property or land values from the Mirrlees Review, the IFS, John Muellbauer, and the Fairer Share campaign.

PolicyEngine UK estimates gross council tax (the amount households owe before any rebate) at £61.4 billion a year for 2026-27, around £1,920 per UK household on average. The OBR's March 2026 forecast puts net council tax receipts at £53.7 billion for the same year. The model estimates average bills range from £1,636 in the bottom income decile to £2,500 in the top.

This post uses PolicyEngine UK, an open-source household-level microsimulation, to model one option researchers have put forward: replacing council tax with a flat land value tax (LVT). The model runs at any rate.

What we find

  • At a budget-neutral 0.83% rate, 71% of households have higher net income. The bottom income decile gains £864 a year on average; the ninth decile pays £1,205 more (the largest average cash loss), and the top decile pays £1,163 more.
  • 0.83% on all UK land value raises the same £61.4 billion that council tax does today, holding aggregate revenue constant.
  • Before housing costs relative poverty falls by 0.49 percentage points; the Gini coefficient on income rises by 0.45%.

The model

We run this analysis with PolicyEngine UK (version 2.88.10), an open-source microsimulation, on Enhanced Family Resources Survey 2023-24 microdata. On the land side, the model gives each household a directly held land-value component and distributes aggregate corporate land to households in proportion to their corporate wealth. We calibrate the total to the ONS National Balance Sheet series AN.211 (2024 target: £7.10 trillion) and uprate to 2026-27 using per-capita nominal GDP growth from the OBR March 2025 Economic and Fiscal Outlook, producing a £7.44 trillion total: £5.38tn held directly by households and £2.06tn through the corporate sector.

On the council tax side, each household has a council tax band (A–H, plus I in Wales) from the FRS. About a quarter of FRS households report their annual bill directly; for the rest, we impute the mean reported bill for the household's region × band × single-person-status cell. We model Northern Ireland's domestic rates separately, and they remain in place under this reform. We then calibrate household weights against VOA stock-of-properties counts of dwellings by band and region (Scotland from the Scottish Government chargeable-dwellings dataset) and against OBR council tax receipts. The 2026-27 council tax baseline this post replaces is £61.4 billion.

The model reports revenue, distributional, poverty, and inequality outcomes at any LVT rate, for every income decile and family type. All figures cover the 2026-27 financial year.

The reform

The reform abolishes council tax across Great Britain and replaces it with a flat LVT on all UK land value at the rate that holds aggregate council tax revenue constant. Northern Ireland's domestic rates remain in place; NI households therefore face the LVT on top of their unchanged rates bill. The LVT applies to all land, including the £2.06 trillion held through the corporate sector, so commercial land contributes alongside residential, while business rates on commercial property also stay in place. For distributional results, the model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners: directly for household-held land, and indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land. No other taxes or benefits change.

Revenue and rate sensitivity

PolicyEngine UK estimates £61.4 billion per year from the current council tax. Dividing that by £7.44 trillion of UK land value gives the budget-neutral rate of 0.83%. Table 1 reports outcomes at three illustrative rates: 0.5%, 0.83%, and 1.0%.

Below the budget-neutral rate, revenue falls and the share of households with higher net income rises. At 0.5%, the model raises £24 billion less than current council tax and 82% of households gain. Above the budget-neutral rate, revenue rises and the share of gainers falls. At 1.0%, the reform raises £13bn more and 65% gain. We hold the rate range to 0.5%-1.0% because the static model assigns the first-round burden to current landowners and does not capture land-price capitalisation, rent pass-through, or behavioural responses, all of which matter more at higher annual rates.

For context, the baseline 2026-27 Gini coefficient on income is 0.38, while the Gini on household total wealth is 0.71. Wealth in the UK is far more concentrated than income, with no considerable change under the reform.

Same reform, by wealth decile

Wealth deciles cut the same simulation along a different axis. Figure 5 plots the average net income change at the 0.83% LVT, by household wealth decile rather than income decile.

Figure 5: Average net income change by wealth decile, 2026-27 (£/year)

The wealth-decile picture is cleaner than the income-decile one. The bottom seven wealth deciles all gain (between £480 and £1,519 a year on average); only deciles 8, 9, and 10 lose, with the top wealth decile paying £6,139 more on average, a larger swing than the £1,163 average loss for the top income decile. The reform is, by construction, a tax on land wealth. It lands progressively along the wealth distribution even when the income-decile pattern is noisier.

The Mirrlees Review and the Fairer Share proposal both discuss deferral mechanisms (letting households accrue the LVT charge against the property until sale or inheritance) as one design response for the income-poor, land-rich households the income-decile averages obscure. PolicyEngine UK can score any of these designs (deferrals, transitional caps, age-based reliefs, or income-tested reductions) with the same distributional metrics; however, we exclude them for this analysis.

Who gains and loses, within deciles

Decile averages hide variation within deciles. Figure 6 reports the share of households with higher, lower, or unchanged net income at the 0.83% rate, by income decile.

Figure 6: Share of households with higher, lower, and unchanged net income by income decile, 0.83% LVT replacing council tax, 2026-27 (%)

In the top decile, 48% of households still have higher net income, despite the average loss of £1,163; in the bottom decile, 10% have lower net income, despite the average gain of £864. The share of losing households is around 10-25% in deciles 1-4 and rises through deciles 5-10, jumping past half in deciles 9 and 10. The same high-land-value, lower-income households that pull decile 4's average into the red also account for the losers in deciles 1–7.

What this model doesn't capture

The static model assigns the first-round LVT burden to current landowners (directly for household-held land, indirectly via corporate equity for corporate land), and holds everything else constant. It does not model land-price capitalisation; rent pass-through to tenants; behavioural responses in land use and development decisions; or the redesign of devolved local-government funding that any reform would require. The model also abstracts from implementation: who values the land, how often, and how to resolve disputes.

Each is a model extension, not an alternative analysis. The directions PolicyEngine UK is investigating: fuller local-authority calibration; capitalisation modelling that tracks burden over time; transitional designs (deferrals, phased introduction, age- or income-based protection); and rent-pass-through under alternative supply-elasticity assumptions.

Using the model

PolicyEngine UK is open source. The model scores specific designs (phased introduction, pensioner deferrals, multi-rate by region, alternative bases) in the same format as this analysis. The code and underlying data are public, and anyone can reproduce these results.
